JYThe shape of the city

Tokyo looks endless on a map. Almost everything a visitor wants sits on or near one rail loop, the JR Yamanote line. It has about 30 stations and takes roughly an hour to go all the way around.

  • West side. Shinjuku has skyscrapers, nightlife and Shinjuku Gyoen garden. Shibuya has the famous crossing and youth shopping. Harajuku, Omotesando and Meiji Shrine sit between them.
  • East side. Tokyo Station, Marunouchi and Ginza surround the Imperial Palace. Akihabara is just north. Ueno is the museum district, with Tokyo National Museum, the zoo and the park.
  • Old Tokyo, east of the loop. Asakusa and Sensō-ji temple, with Skytree across the river.
  • The bay, southeast. Toyosu has teamLab Planets and the fish market. Tsukiji's outer market is the better place to eat. Odaiba is a short ride from either.
  • "Tokyo" is 23 city wards plus suburbs and islands. Very little of what you came for is outside the wards, apart from day trips.

N03Your corner: Shirokane

Shirokane is quiet, upscale and residential, in the southern middle of Minato ward. Expect few tourists, good restaurants and calm evenings. Your station is Shirokane-takanawa N03 I03, about a 7-minute walk.

Namboku line. North to Azabu-Juban N04, Roppongi-itchome N05 and Tameike-sanno N06, where you change to the Ginza line for Ginza or Asakusa.
Mita line. North to Mita I04 for the Asakusa line and the airport train, then on to Otemachi I09 and Jimbocho I10.
Ōedo line at Azabu-Juban, one stop north. It loops through Roppongi, Shinjuku and Tsukiji.

OHDay trips for your interests

Each name opens a live route. Sample trains below were read from Yahoo Transit on 18 October for Wednesday 21 October; the pattern holds on other days but check the day before. Late October is leaf season, so the first train out beats the crowds everywhere on this list.

Hakone, for the sculpture park and hot springs

Getting there. The Odakyu Romancecar runs from Shinjuku to Hakone-Yumoto in about 75 to 85 minutes, all reserved seats, roughly two an hour from early morning. Book the seat on Odakyu's e-Romancecar site or at the Shinjuku counter the day before; the seat charge is on top of the fare. Shinjuku is 30 minutes from the Airbnb, so leaving home at 7:00 gets you into Hakone before 9:30.

The loop. Buy the two-day Hakone Freepass at Shinjuku (about ¥7,100, confirm) and it covers everything after the Romancecar: the Tozan mountain railway with its switchbacks, the cable car, the ropeway over Owakudani's sulphur vents, the pirate ship across Lake Ashi and the buses back. The Hakone Open-Air Museum is at Chokoku-no-Mori station on the Tozan line, open 9:00 to 17:00 every day, about ¥2,000 or a little less online, with the Picasso pavilion, a Henry Moore field and a hot-spring foot bath among the sculptures. Do it first, before the loop fills up, and allow two hours. Fuji shows from the ropeway and the lake on a clear morning.

Back. Reserve the return Romancecar when you book the outbound; the last ones leave in the evening and fill on weekends. If you miss it, the Tozan train to Odawara and a Shinkansen or local JR train still gets you to Shinjuku, with a sample last connection at 22:21 from Hakone-Yumoto.

Nikko, for the national park and the shrines

Getting there. Tobu limited expresses leave Asakusa for Tobu-Nikko in just under two hours, all reserved; the sample 8:30 Revaty on 21 October arrives 10:23 with one platform change at Shimo-Imaichi. Book on Tobu's site; the newer Spacia X sells out first. From the Airbnb, Mita line to Mita and the Asakusa line straight to Asakusa is about 35 minutes with one change, so a 7:40 start works.

The day. Toshogu shrine, Rinnoji temple and the red Shinkyo bridge are a short bus ride from the station and make a full day on their own. Toshogu has long flights of stone steps; the 200-plus to Ieyasu's tomb are optional. The World Heritage Area bus pass sold at the station covers the loop. Lake Chuzenji and Kegon Falls are higher up the Irohazaka hairpin road, which gridlocks in leaf season; in late October go up only if you're on the first buses, or save it.

Back. Book the return express too. Last trains out of Tobu-Nikko are around 21:00 and the JR alternative via Utsunomiya runs a little later; the sample 20:57 departure reaches Asakusa at 23:54.

    Sculpture, in order of payoff

    • Hakone Open-Air Museum is the one worth a whole day: Moore, Miró, Niki de Saint Phalle, Picasso's own pavilion, all on a hillside with hot springs below. It never closes. The plan is in Day trips.
    • Rodin at the National Museum of Western Art. The Gates of Hell, The Thinker and The Burghers of Calais stand in the front courtyard in Ueno, free, with Le Corbusier's building behind them. Combine with the Tokyo National Museum across the park on any day but Monday.
    • Maman. Louise Bourgeois's spider is outside Roppongi Hills, free, two stops from the base. Good at night.
    • Yayoi Kusama Museum is small, five floors, Thursday to Sunday only, and sells timed tickets on its website on the first of each month. If October is sold out, her pumpkins and dots also turn up at the Mori and National Art Center shops and, this trip, likely nowhere else in the city.
    • Nezu Museum is the quiet one: a Kengo Kuma building, bronzes and ceramics inside, and a garden with stone lanterns and tea houses that is at its best in late October.
    • 21_21 Design Sight and the National Art Center are a ten-minute walk apart in Roppongi and both close on Tuesday. Neither has a permanent collection, so check the show before you go.

    Worth knowing

    • The Mori Art Museum is between exhibitions. Ron Mueck closed 23 September and Mariko Mori opens 31 October, the day after you fly. Past gaps have meant closed galleries, so treat it as closed unless the website says otherwise. The observation deck and shop in the same tower run on their own schedule.
    • The Teien Art Museum is your neighbourhood museum, a ten-minute walk, an Art Deco prince's house with a garden and, right now, the Marimekko printmaking show. The easiest museum outing on the list for older relatives.
    • teamLab needs tickets before you go. Both venues are timed entry, sold only on the official site, and the popular slots go a week or more out. Borderless at Azabudai Hills is the closer one and the better one for a first visit; Planets is the one with water.
    • Big museums are cheap, usually ¥1,000 to ¥2,000. teamLab is the exception at ¥3,400 and up.
    • Friday and Saturday evenings are when the Ueno museums stay open late and the crowds thin.

    遊Amusement parks

    Tokyo Disney Resort is the main event and it is in Halloween mode for your whole stay, which runs to 31 October. Minutes are door-to-door from the Airbnb.

    Tokyo Disney Resort, 45 minutes

    Which park. DisneySea exists nowhere else in the world and is the one for adults: harbour and volcano scenery, better food, Fantasy Springs with the Frozen and Tangled lands. Disneyland is the familiar one and the better choice if small children are along. One park per ticket; a day each if you have two days.

    Tickets. Dated, sold on the official site or app, with prices that move by date; October weekends run to about ¥12,400 for an adult and weekdays nearer ¥9,000. Buy now for any date in your window; weekends do sell out. Download the app before you go: it holds the ticket, lets you take a free Standby Pass for the busiest attractions and buy Premier Access, a paid skip-the-line per ride, which is worth it for one or two headliners. Arrive 45 minutes before opening.

    Halloween. Adult costumes are allowed on set days in the second half of October; the parade and the decorations are the draw, and the parks are busier than usual.

    Getting there. Mita line to Otemachi or Hibiya, then to Tokyo Station and the JR Keiyo line to Maihama, about 45 minutes with two changes. The Keiyo platforms at Tokyo Station are a long underground walk; allow 10 minutes.

    Used cameras and lenses

    Map Camera is the one to go to: a multi-floor shop a few minutes from Shinjuku's west exit, the largest used-camera dealer in Japan, with condition grades you can trust, a one-year warranty on used gear, tax-free and English-speaking staff.     Pokémon cards: Pokémon Centers for the exclusives, Akihabara for the cards

    Two different trips. The Pokémon Centers sell new sealed product at list price, promo cards, the centre-exclusive goods and the plush, and almost no singles. Japanese packs are cheap at list: ¥200 for a five-card pack of the current sets, ¥360 for the 30th-anniversary set, and a 30-pack box is thirty times the pack price with no premium. The catch is that each new set sells by lottery through Pokémon Center Online, weighted towards residents who have verified a My Number card, and the shelves carry per-person caps that change with every release, so buy what is on the shelf and do not plan a box around a Pokémon Center. Four in Tokyo: Tokyo DX on the fifth floor of Nihombashi Takashimaya's east building, 10:30 to 21:00, with the Pokémon Cafe beside it (book online); Shibuya on the sixth floor of Shibuya Parco, 10:00 to 21:00, sharing the floor with Nintendo Tokyo and the Capcom Store; Mega Tokyo on the second floor of Sunshine City in Ikebukuro, 10:00 to 20:00, the biggest and the one with the Pokémon Card Station play area, closed from March to August this year and still limiting entry, so expect a queue; and Skytree Town on the fourth floor of Solamachi, 10:00 to 21:00. The small Pokémon Store on Character Street inside Tokyo Station is the one you pass anyway, open to 20:30.

    Singles, older cards and anything graded live in Akihabara, all within five minutes of the Electric Town exit. Hareruya2 Akihabara Tower (Ishimaru No. 3 building, three minutes from the exit) is seven floors of nothing but Pokémon cards, basement to sixth, 10:00 to 22:00 every day: current sets, bargain singles, supplies, 152 free play seats, and a buying counter that takes everything including kyū-ura, the 1996 to 2001 Japanese cards with the old card back, which is what "vintage" means here; its Annex on the same block runs to 23:00. Card Rush has the sharpest single prices and the longest queues: shop 2 on floors 1 and 2 of the Omori building and shop 0 on the seventh floor of Bito Akiba Plaza, weekdays 13:00 to 21:00, weekends from 11:00 (shop 1 is temporarily closed). Fukufuku Toreka in the Kimura building, Pokémon on the second floor, weekdays 13:00 to 20:45, weekends from 10:00, is the name collectors give for older and high-value cards. magi, fifth floor at Sotokanda 3-14-9 with a branch in Radio Kaikan, deals in high-value and graded cards (PSA slabs and the Japanese grader ARS), cash or QR pay only, strict on condition. Radio Kaikan itself, the tower beside the station, 10:00 to 20:00, holds eight card shops on floors 1, 2, 7 and 9 plus Yellow Submarine on 6 and magi on 5, so start there if time is short. For genuinely old stock also try Mandarake's Card-kan on the second floor of Nakano Broadway, 12:00 to 20:00: Carddass, old stickers, phone cards and early Pokémon alongside the newer games.

    House rules: photography inside card shops is usually banned, opening packs in the shop depends on the shop, small shops prefer cash, and selling needs a passport and sometimes Japanese residency. Walk past anything labelled "replica" or "metal card" and the lucky-dip oripa packs. Whether cards count as tax-free goods is not written in the official categories, so ask at the till; Hareruya2 and Card Rush do not say on their sites. Before a big card, compare the shop's price with its own online listing; the Japanese market, not eBay, sets the number.

    Retro games

    Akihabara again, all within ten minutes' walk of each other.     How shopping works here

    • Tax-free needs your passport and ¥5,000 or more at one shop in one day. The shop takes the tax off at the till and seals consumables in a bag you must not open in Japan. Look for the "Tax-Free" sign. A refund-at-the-airport system is scheduled to replace this from November 2026, after your trip.
    • Shops open late and close early. Most open at 10:30 or 11:00 and close at 20:00. Department stores are strict about 20:00. Don Quijote and convenience stores are the exceptions.
    • Cash still matters at small shops, markets and the used-book district. Department stores and chains take cards and phone wallets.
    • Bags cost a few yen and staff will ask. Carry a tote.
    • Sizes run small. Shoe sizes are in centimetres. Try things on; returns are rare.
    • Buy a suitcase here rather than hauling an empty one. Don Quijote and the Ginza department stores sell them, then ship the full bag to Narita by takkyubin from any convenience store two days before you fly.
    • Post offices ship abroad and sell the boxes. Heavy ceramics are cheaper to post than to carry.
    • Knives and anything sharp go in checked bags. Big electronics are often cheaper at home once you add the voltage question; small things like earphones and cameras are the good buys.

    ¥How dining works here

    Getting a table

    • Lunch is the bargain. The same kitchen often charges half at lunch. Omakase counters that run ¥18,000 at night may do a ¥3,000 lunch set.
    • Small counters are reservation only and book out a week or more ahead. Ask the Airbnb host to call, or use TableCheck or Omakase.in, which take bookings in English for many places.
    • No-shows are a serious offence. Cancel by phone if plans change. Some counters charge the full course for a no-show.
    • Ramen, soba and set-meal shops don't take bookings. Queue, and expect to be in and out in 30 minutes. Many have a ticket machine by the door: pay first, hand over the ticket.
    • Restaurants close between services, roughly 14:30 to 17:30, and many close one weekday. Check the closed day before you set out.
    • Groups over four are hard at counters. Book ahead, or pick yakiniku, shabu-shabu or izakaya, which seat tables.

    At the table

    • Izakaya add a cover charge, called otoshi, with a small appetiser you didn't order. Around ¥300–800 a head. That's normal.
    • Don't tip. It confuses people. The bill is the bill.
    • Say "sumimasen" to call staff. Nobody will come otherwise. Many places have a call button on the table.
    • Omakase etiquette: eat each piece in one bite as it's served, soy sauce is already applied, and ginger is a palate cleanser between pieces, not a topping. No perfume. Ask before taking photos.
    • Allergies and dietary limits go in the booking, not to the chef mid-course. Vegetarian is hard at sushi and yakitori; shabu-shabu, tempura, soba and set-meal places cope better. Dashi broth almost always contains fish.
    • Slurping noodles is fine. Blowing your nose at the table is not.

    Paying

    • Pay at the register by the door, not at the table. Take the bill up when you're done.
    • Carry cash. Small restaurants, ticket-machine shops, shrines and market stalls may not take cards. 7-Eleven ATMs accept foreign cards.
    • Bring your passport for tax-free shopping at participating stores on larger purchases.

    揉Massage and baths

    Japan treats a massage as maintenance, not a treat, so it is cheap, everywhere and open late. There are three kinds, and knowing which door you are walking through saves money and surprises.

    The three kinds

    • Relaxation chains (リラクゼーション). The shopfronts in every station building and mall: Raffine, Re.Ra.Ku, Karada Factory. Momihogushi is a firm kneading done through your clothes, with loungewear provided; the therapists are trained by the chain rather than state-licensed. Roughly ¥2,300 for 20 minutes and ¥7,000 to 8,000 for an hour. Walk in or book the same day. This is the one to use after a long shopping day.
    • Licensed shiatsu and anma (指圧・あんま). Finger-pressure therapy by practitioners with a national licence, usually in a clinic rather than a salon. Better for a bad back or a stiff neck that a chain cannot shift. Expect ¥7,000 to 11,000 for 60 to 90 minutes and book a day ahead.
    • Head spa, oil and Thai. Head spa (a long scalp, neck and shoulder treatment, often with a wash) is the current Tokyo craze and Azabu-Juban has several salons; oil and aroma courses are undressed under towels; Thai places cluster around Roppongi and Azabu-Juban. Prices run from chain level up to ¥12,000 or more for 40 minutes at the boutique ones.

Wayanpuri advertises English menus; onsenSPA annex runs 10:00 to 06:00 at ¥11,000 to 12,000 for a 40-minute dry head spa. Book through the salon's own page or ask the host to phone.

    • Hotel spas. The Aman, Mandarin Oriental and Peninsula spas are the luxury end, several times the chain prices and booked days ahead. Worth it once if you want the view and the pool with it; not for a sore back.

    Baths: onsen and sento

    A sento is the neighbourhood public bath, ¥550 by law in Tokyo; an onsen is fed by a hot spring; a super sento is a bath complex with saunas, restaurants and massage inside. All three are the best thing to do with a tired body in this city.

    • Takenoyu, Minami-Azabu. Maps ↗ A real hot-spring sento ten minutes' walk from Azabu-Juban, with dark tea-coloured water from its own source, even in the cold plunge. 15:30 to 23:30, closed Mondays and Fridays, ¥550 cash, sauna extra. Bring your own towel and soap or buy them at the counter. Tattoo rule not published; ask at the desk.
    • Shimizu-yu, Minami-Aoyama. Maps ↗ A modern sento two minutes from Omotesando exit A4 (19 minutes from the base): soft water throughout, a strong carbonated bath and a silk bath, about ¥1,100 with the sauna. 12:00 to 24:00, to 23:00 at weekends, closed Fridays. It refuses tattoos, including henna.
    • Thermae-Yu, Shinjuku. Maps ↗ The super sento, two minutes from Shinjuku-sanchome exit E1 and open 24 hours: baths, saunas, a rock bath, restaurants and a floor of body care, foot care, head spa and Thai treatment. ¥3,100 Monday to Thursday and ¥3,200 Friday to Sunday for up to 18 hours, loungewear and towels included; staying past midnight adds about ¥2,000. Tattoos: foreign visitors are admitted with a ¥330 cover sticker and a passport; small tattoos on women need no sticker; it refuses tattooed Japanese men.

    How it goes

    • At a massage: shoes off at the door, phone off, the course sheet is pictures. Say kata for shoulders, koshi for lower back, mō sukoshi tsuyoku for harder and yowaku for softer; itai means it hurts and they will ease off at once. Pay after, card or cash at the chains. No tipping anywhere in this chapter.
    • At a bath: undress completely in the changing room, wash and rinse at a seated tap before you get in, keep the small towel out of the water (on your head is traditional), tie long hair up, no photos, speak quietly. Soak ten minutes, cool off, repeat. Drink water after. Men and women bathe separately.
    • Tattoos are still refused at many baths; the three above tell you where they stand. A sento is usually more relaxed than a resort onsen, and a waterproof cover sticker from a pharmacy solves it at the places that allow covering.

    UDAccessibility

    Tokyo is kind to slow walkers once you stop treating the train network as a sport. This chapter is for anyone in the party who moves slowly or with help: a cane, a bad knee, a wheelchair, a stroller, or simply a long day. The rules were written for the days the older relatives are along, and they hold for everyone.

    What the operators provide

    • Toei (Mita, Oedo, Asakusa, Shinjuku lines). Elevators at every station give at least one step-free route from street to gate to platform, and every station keeps a portable ramp, so staff can bridge the train gap at any point on the platform. Ask at the gate; they phone ahead so someone meets the train at the other end.
    • Tokyo Metro (Namboku, Hibiya, Ginza and the rest). Its own figures put step-free routes at every station by this fiscal year, but at the big interchanges such as Ginza and Shibuya there may be only one or two elevators, often far from the exit you want. Plan the elevator exit, not the nearest one.
    • Ticket gates and toilets. The wide gate is the one beside the staffed booth. Multipurpose toilets with grab rails are at almost every station and in every department store and museum.
    • Wheelchair taxis. Most Tokyo cabs are the boxy JPN Taxi with a sliding door and a fold-out ramp. For a guaranteed one, request Uber Assist in the Uber app (23 wards): a certified driver helps with boarding, folds a chair or walker into the back, and charges the ordinary metered fare plus any dispatch fee. Chairs up to 120 by 70 cm fit.

    Pace

    • One anchor a day, with a sit-down lunch in the middle and home by 16:00, before the evening rush. A museum and its café is a full day. Two neighbourhoods is too many.
    • Build in chairs. Department stores have lounges on the upper floors, museums have cafés, parks have benches. Shinjuku Gyoen and the Teien garden are the gentlest walks on this list.
    • Mornings are better than afternoons for crowds, weather and energy.

    Trains and taxis

    • Every station has an elevator route, but it may be far from the platform you arrive on. Look for the elevator symbol on the station map at the ticket gate, or ask staff for the erebētā. Namboku line platforms have doors and the cars are new.
    • Avoid 7:30–9:30 and 17:30–19:30. No seats, no space for a cane or a chair. Priority seats and the wheelchair bays are at the car ends; people do give them up if asked.
    • Taxis are the right answer more often than you'd think. The GO app works in English and takes cards; Uber also works here and sends ordinary taxis. Fares start around ¥500 and a ride from Shirokane to Ginza or Roppongi lands roughly in the ¥2,000–3,000 range. Doors open and close themselves, so don't touch them. Taxi ranks sit outside every department store and big hotel.
    • For the day trips, Hakone is the gentle one: a reserved seat from Shinjuku, no changes, then the Open-Air Museum, which has slopes but paths everywhere and places to sit. Skip the ropeway queue. Nikko's shrines are steep stone steps; Kamakura's Great Buddha is flat and the tram is fun.

    Eating with a group

    • Restaurants are the weak point: many sit up or down a narrow stair with no accessible toilet, so favour ground-floor and department-store dining when a chair or a bad hip is along.
    • Use the Seating filter under Where to eat: every restaurant row now carries its seat count, whether it has tables, a counter, a sunken floor or tatami, private rooms, and whether it lists wheelchair access, all from the restaurant's own Tabelog entry.
    • Pick table restaurants, not counters. From the list in this guide: the yakiniku places (Ushimatsu, Kiraku Tei, Yakiniku Jumbo, Niku Yakiya D-29, KABUN), the shabu-shabu and sukiyaki places (Kotakino, Sukiyaki Juniten, Sugino, Kabuto), the set-meal lunches and the "familiar" group (Azabu Kogaiken, Edoya, AMANOYA). Omakase sushi and the tasting-menu yakitori are eight-seat counters with fixed courses, which is a lot to ask of someone who doesn't love raw fish.
    • Book for five or more, even at lunch. Say the number and the time; the Airbnb host can call.
    • Shabu-shabu and sukiyaki are the crowd-pleasers: cooked at the table, mild, and everyone chooses what to eat. Kotakino is a short walk from the Airbnb.
    • Chairs, not floor seating. Some traditional rooms are tatami with low tables. Ask for a table seat, tēburu seki, when booking.

    Practical

    • Toilets are everywhere and clean: every station, convenience store, department store and park. Western-style with heated seats is the norm.
    • Luggage goes by courier, not by hand. Any convenience store or the Airbnb host can send bags to the airport or a hotel by takkyubin for a modest fee; allow two days for Narita. Station lockers big enough for a suitcase are scarce.
    • Pharmacies with a green cross sign are on most streets, and see the Help section for the hospital nearest the Airbnb with English-speaking staff.
    • Bring prescriptions in their original packaging with a copy of the script. Some common US medicines, including pseudoephedrine decongestants and ADHD stimulants, are restricted in Japan.

    礼Manners that matter

    • No phone calls on trains, and keep your voice low.
    • Eating while walking is frowned upon in most places. Market streets are the exception.
    • Trash cans are rare. Carry a small bag for rubbish.
    • Wear slip-on shoes and decent socks. Temples, traditional inns and some restaurants ask you to take shoes off.
    • Ignore street touts in Roppongi and Kabukicho. Some bars they lead you to run up huge bills.

    Late October is usually mild and good for walking. It is also the tail of typhoon season, so check train status before a day trip.
